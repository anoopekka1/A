01

Think like a cataloguer

Foundations, canons and principles · Parts A–C

Concepts

What you will understand

The CCC is not just a list of isolated formatting rules. Ranganathan explains its development and frames rules through canons and normative principles. When you meet a difficult title page, identify the cataloguing problem first, then find the relevant rule and defend your decision.

Learn these ideas

  • Ascertainability (BB): use information on the title page and its overflow for the sections governed by the canon; some heading decisions may require additional evidence under the relevant rule.
  • Prepotence (BC): concentrate as much filing power as possible in the leading section/entry element so the entry can be found in its proper place.
  • Individualisation (BD): a heading should identify one entity; add the necessary and sufficient identifying elements when names are homonyms.
  • Sought-heading (BE): consider whether a reader is likely to look for a document under a particular kind, choice or rendering of heading before creating an added entry.
  • Context (BF), Permanence (BG), Currency (BH), Consistence (BJ): interpret a detail in its context; avoid arbitrary changes to established name headings; use current terminology for subject headings within the canon's scope; and keep treatment consistent.
  • General principles (Part C): the Laws of Library Science, Laws of Interpretation, Impartiality, Symmetry, Parsimony, Local Variation and Osmosis help explain how rules are interpreted and kept practical.

Practical assignment

Take three ambiguous cases: same author name as another person, a subject name that has changed over time, and a title-page that mentions a sponsor. For each, write: (1) the decision required, (2) the canon that may apply, (3) the exact chapter/section you would verify. Do not invent a rule from the canon alone.

Quick check: two different people share the same name. Which canon most directly addresses making the heading identify one and only one entity?

Source anchor: Part A, pp. 17–32; Part B, pp. 33–56; Part C, pp. 57–76. Particularly BB–BJ and CA–CG.
02

Understand the catalogue and the card

Catalogue functions, recording and entry vocabulary · Parts D–F

Structure

Build the mental model

Part D explains what a catalogue does, its parts and physical forms, the reader's approach, and centralised cataloguing. Part E explains recording: language and script, articles and contractions, style, book size, continuation cards and how entries are arranged in classified and alphabetical parts.

Part F gives the vocabulary needed to discuss a real document precisely: work, title, author, collaborator, sponsor, document, kinds of documents, preliminary pages, series, related documents and edition. It then defines catalogue and entry types, the Classified Catalogue, entry sections and headings.

Know these distinctions

  • A work is not automatically the same as the physical document containing it.
  • A collaborator contributes in a secondary capacity; a sponsor supports publication but is not automatically the author.
  • A main entry is the specific entry giving maximum information about the whole document; other entries are normally derived from it.
  • The book defines the leading section, heading section, title section, note section, annotation section, accession-number section and tracing section. Do not treat these labels as interchangeable.

Practice

Use a real book. Identify the title page and overflow, preliminary pages, edition, series, collaborators and sponsor separately. Then draw a blank card before filling it in. Keep physical description/imprint information in the appropriate bibliographical area; only enter elements the applicable rule calls for.

Quick check: in CCC terminology, which section on the back of a main-entry card indicates the added entries for the document?

Source anchor: Part D, pp. 77–90; Part E, pp. 91–115; Part F, pp. 117–180. Key chapters include FN (Entry), FQ (Sections of Entry) and FR (Heading).
03

Decide authorship and render headings

Authorship conflicts, personal names and heading forms · Parts G–J

Decision rules

Start with responsibility, not just the cover

Part G addresses conflicts such as person versus person, person versus corporate body, and corporate body versus corporate body. Part H studies personal-name forms, including simple and compound names, split given names, forms of a person's name, Arabic names, and cases of illusion and solution. Part J covers the rendering of personal names, geographical names, governments, institutions, conferences, titles, series and pseudo-series.

Rules of reasoning

  • Do not assume the most prominent name is automatically the author. Ask who is responsible for the thought/expression and whether the work belongs to a corporate body's function.
  • A name in the imprint as publisher/sponsor is not by itself enough to prove corporate authorship. Consult the conflict-of-authorship rules.
  • Distinguish joint authorship from a collaborator's secondary contribution. The author and collaborator may both be access points, but the code treats their roles differently.
  • When selecting and rendering a heading, use the correct chapter for the type of entity. The choice of heading and its exact rendering are related but separate decisions.
  • For names, homonyms and variant forms, use the code's individualising and cross-reference provisions rather than making an improvised form.

Practice cases

  1. A lecture is delivered by one person and notes are compiled by another.
  2. A university committee issues an administrative report that also names an officer.
  3. A book has two joint authors, then another book has four credited authors.
  4. A book series has a common name that could match another series.

For each, determine which chapter family applies and record the rule section you need to consult before making the card.

Quick check: a person translates a work written by someone else. Which role should you investigate first under Part F's terminology and the associated rules?

Source anchor: Part G, pp. 181–204; Part H, pp. 205–240; Part J, pp. 241–278. Read the relevant chapter—not this summary alone—for actual name-rendering decisions.
04

Master the Class Index Entry

Classification terms, chain procedure and class-index headings · Part K

Core skill

The big idea

In a Classified Catalogue, the Class Index Entry (CIE) is a general added word-entry that refers from the name of a class to its class number. It helps a reader who starts with the subject name find the class number and then the documents in the Classified Part.

The code derives CIEs through the Chain Procedure from the class number of the Main Entry or a Cross Reference Entry. The chain is not simply “make an entry for every number fragment.” Learn the code's terminology of classification, its rules for choosing and rendering a class-index heading, and how links are treated in examples.

Key terms to learn

  • Class number: the class identifier used to locate the class in the classified catalogue.
  • Chain and link: the class-number path and the subject concepts represented at its successive points.
  • Sought, unsought and false links: categories explicitly discussed in the book's chain examples; learn how the code treats each rather than assuming every link produces the same type of entry.
  • Choice vs rendering: deciding which class-index heading to use is different from deciding the form in which that heading is written.
  • Special cases: the Part K contents also include classic works, commission reports, homonyms in class-index headings, feature headings and dictionary-catalogue modifications.

Live card practice

Use the studio below to enter a heading, directing statement and class number. The sample PHYSICS. — C and MATHEMATICS. — B are training examples already seen in your companion handout; the rule method and the extended syllabus map here follow the 5th-edition book. Always consult Part K for the exact chain result on a real class number.

05

Build main entries and their added entries

Cross-reference index entries, main entry and book index entries · Parts L–M

Card craft

One document, linked entries

The Main Entry gives maximum information about a document. Other entries normally derive from it, but different entries solve different reader-search problems. The Main Entry's tracing section identifies the added entries prepared for the document.

  • Main-entry front: leading section, heading, title section and the other sections required by the kind of document; the accession-number section is at the end of the front.
  • Tracing section: on the back of the Main Entry card; use it to account for the added entries prepared.
  • Cross Reference Entry (classified catalogue): a specific entry that relates a document to its location/subject classification according to the rule.
  • Book Index Entry (BIE): a specific added word-entry for a particular document; its heading gives an access point and its following section specifies the document, with a call number/index-number section.
  • Cross Reference Index Entry (CRIE): a general added entry referring from an alternative or related word/form to the preferred heading, such as alternative-name, variant-form, pseudonym/real-name, editor-of-series or generic-name cases.

How to work a book

  1. Inspect the title page and overflow, preliminary pages and relevant notes.
  2. Decide the authorship conflict and main heading under Parts G–J.
  3. Write each main-entry section using the relevant Part M rule and the Code's style.
  4. Ask which added entries are required and likely to be sought; verify under Parts K, L and M.
  5. Record those entries in the tracing section; then draft each entry separately and check that its wording and card layout satisfy the specific rule.

In the companion PDF on card skeletons, the tracing area is diagrammed with class index, book index and cross-reference index areas. Treat that visual as a layout aid; the governing entry definitions are in Parts K–M of this edition.

Quick check: which entry type is described as a specific added word-entry that points to a particular document?

Source anchor: Part L, pp. 337–354; Part M, pp. 355–412. Key Part M chapters: MA–MH (Main Entry), MJ (Cross Reference Entry), MK (Book Index Entry).
06

Handle composite, multi-volume and periodical works

Complex documents and changing publication patterns · Parts N and P

Edge cases

Do not treat every book as a simple monograph

Part N covers ordinary composite books, artificial composite books and multi-volumed books. A composite book may contain distinct contributions, each with its own title; in an ordinary composite work, a generic title brings the contributions together, while an artificial composite book may have no common title-page/title for the combined contents. Determine the document type before choosing a rule.

Part P covers periodical-publication complexities: simple periodicals, irregularity in volume-number, interrupted publication, change of title and sponsor, amalgamation, splitting up, supplements, combinations of complexities, dictionary-catalogue alternatives and desiderata.

Workflow for complex cases

  1. Describe the publication history: one work in several volumes, multiple independent contributions, or a continuing publication?
  2. Record what actually changed: title, sponsor, volume numbering, publication continuity, merge/split or supplement.
  3. Locate the exact chapter within N or P before making the Main Entry or related entries.
  4. Keep the old and new relationships visible as the particular rules require; do not invent a generic “new edition” treatment for every change.

The terminology chapter distinguishes a periodical, made up of distinct contributions within a knowledge area, from a serial, which generally repeats a kind of information in a similar pattern over successive periods. Use the source's definitions and the relevant P rule when a real title falls near the boundary.

Quick check: a periodical changes title and sponsor. Which Part should you consult first for its serial-specific cataloguing treatment?

Source anchor: Part N, pp. 413–432; Part P, pp. 433–488, including PA–PJ and its dictionary-catalogue/desiderata sections.
07

Connect CCC to the wider bibliographic system

Union catalogues, national bibliography, indexes, abstracts and capstone · Parts Q–W

Integration

Finish the complete map

  • Part Q: Union Catalogue of Books — coverage, rule modifications, main entry, library number and union-catalogue card.
  • Part R: Union Catalogue of Periodical Publications — coverage, users, physical form, main/other entries, abbreviations, contractions, typography, layout, pagination and card technique.
  • Part S: National Bibliography — definition, value, physical form, rule modifications, entries and layout of printed unit cards.
  • Part T: Indexing Periodical — value, coverage, necessary rule modifications, main and other entries, classified index and presentation.
  • Part U: Abstracting Periodical — value, canons, personnel, rule modifications, main entry and examples.
  • Part V: incunabula and non-book materials for consideration.
  • Part W: glossary, bibliographical references and index—tools for resolving uncertainty and locating rules.

Capstone: catalogue one real item

  1. Collect title-page/overflow, edition, publication details, collaborators, series and notes.
  2. Make an authorship/heading decision and cite the exact section.
  3. Draft the main-entry front in its sections.
  4. Derive any required class-index entry by the Part K method.
  5. Prepare the required book-index/cross-reference entries under Parts L–M.
  6. Check tracing against the added entries, then review style, arrangement and any special document-type rules.

For a periodical, union catalogue, national bibliography, index or abstract, switch to the relevant Part rather than forcing all cases into the ordinary single-volume-book workflow.

Quick check: the book you are cataloguing is for a national bibliography. Which Part is the first place to check?

Source anchor: Parts Q–W, pp. 489–623. Keep Part W open as a navigation aid during the capstone.
