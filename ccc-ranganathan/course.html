01

Think like a cataloguer

Origins, canons and normative principles · Parts A–C

Foundation

Learning goal

Understand why the CCC is a connected system of rules, not just a set of card-writing conventions. Ranganathan describes a scientific cycle: experience, generalisation, principles, rules, and fresh experience.

The eight canons in Part B

  • Ascertainability (BB): use evidence from the title page and its overflow for the entry sections governed by this canon. Do not assume that every heading question is solved by the title page alone.
  • Prepotence (BC): concentrate filing power in the leading section and entry element as far as possible. Early letters/digits have great influence on where a card is filed.
  • Individualisation (BD): make a heading identify one entity, adding necessary and sufficient identifying elements where names collide.
  • Sought-Heading (BE): ask whether readers are likely to look under this type, choice or rendering of heading, or seek the added entry.
  • Context (BF): interpret a name or detail in the context in which it appears.
  • Permanence (BG): do not arbitrarily change established name headings; the Code defines when changes are appropriate.
  • Currency (BH): use current subject terminology for class-index/subject headings within the canon's scope.
  • Consistence (BJ): apply the Code consistently across like cases.

Part C: principles that guide interpretation

Study the Laws of Library Science and Laws of Interpretation, followed by Impartiality, Symmetry, Parsimony, Local Variation and Osmosis. Parsimony is particularly useful in practice: avoid unnecessary complexity and excessive added entries, while still satisfying the Code's requirements and reader needs.

Practical assignment

Write down three situations: (1) two different people with the same name, (2) a subject whose name changes over time, and (3) a title page that names a sponsor. For each, identify the issue and the canon that may help frame it. Then locate the actual rule before deciding how to catalogue it.

Quick check: two different people share the same name. Which canon most directly addresses making the heading identify one entity?

Source map: Part A, pp. 17–32; Part B, pp. 33–56; Part C, pp. 57–76. Key chapters: BB–BJ and CA–CG.

02

Understand catalogues, cards and terminology

Catalogue functions, recording and entry sections · Parts D–F

Structure

Learn the system before filling the card

Part D explains the functions and parts of a library catalogue, the reader's approach, physical forms and centralised cataloguing. Part E deals with language and script, articles and contractions, style of writing, size of book, continued cards, and arrangement of entries in the classified and alphabetical parts.

Terminology from Part F

  • Work and document: distinguish the intellectual work from the document that embodies it.
  • Author: identify responsibility for the thought and expression of the work; do not assume that every named person has the same role.
  • Collaborator: a person or body associated with a work in a secondary capacity, such as editor, translator, illustrator or reviser, where the applicable rules treat that role as collaboration.
  • Sponsor: a person or body supporting publication; sponsorship by itself does not prove authorship.
  • Series, related documents and edition: identify these separately and consult their terminology/rules rather than collapsing them into a general note.
  • Entry and heading: an entry is a catalogue unit; the heading is the element used to organise/access that entry.

Main-entry sections

The Code defines a leading section, heading section, title section, bibliographical section, locus section, note section, annotation section, accession-number section, tracing section, directing section, second section and index-number section for the applicable entry types. Not every entry contains every section.

For a main-entry card, the front carries its prescribed descriptive sections and accession number; the back carries the tracing section identifying the added entries. The precise contents depend on the type of document and the relevant rule.

Practical assignment

Choose a book and inspect its title page, overflow, preliminary pages and series information. Make notes on the author's role, collaborators, edition, imprint, physical description and series. Label these facts before writing a card.

Quick check: which section on the back of a main-entry card indicates its added entries?

Source map: Part D, pp. 77–90; Part E, pp. 91–115; Part F, pp. 117–180. Key chapters: FN (Entry), FQ (Sections of Entry), FR (Heading).

03

Decide authorship and render headings

Conflicts of authorship, personal names and heading forms · Parts G–J

Decisions

Start with responsibility, not appearance

Part G treats conflicts such as person versus person, person versus corporate body, corporate body versus corporate body, and cases where an original author's name is merged into a title. Part H treats forms of personal names. Part J gives rules for rendering personal and geographical names, governments, institutions, conferences, titles, series and pseudo-series.

A repeatable decision process

  1. Read the title page and its overflow; identify every named person and corporate body, and where each appears.
  2. Determine the nature of the work and who is responsible for its thought/expression.
  3. Separate authorship from secondary contribution, sponsorship, publication and editorial work.
  4. Identify the entity type for the heading: person, geographical entity, government, institution, conference, title or series.
  5. Use the relevant Part G rule to resolve authorship, then the relevant Part H/J rule to choose and render the heading. Check homonyms and variant forms.

Practice cases

Consider a lecture with notes compiled by another person; an administrative report that names an officer; a work by two joint authors; a work credited to three or more authors; and a series with a generic name that can be confused with another. For each, state which chapter family you would inspect before deciding.

Quick check: a person translates a work written by someone else. Which role should you investigate first in the Code's terminology?

Source map: Part G, pp. 181–204; Part H, pp. 205–240; Part J, pp. 241–278.

04

Master the Class Index Entry

Classification terminology, Chain Procedure and CIEs · Part K

Core skill

What a Class Index Entry does

A Class Index Entry (CIE) is a general added word-entry in a Classified Catalogue, referring from a class name to its class number. It lets a reader who starts with the name of a subject find the class number and then the Classified Part of the catalogue.

Chain Procedure

The Code derives CIEs by Chain Procedure from the class number of the Main Entry or of a Cross Reference Entry. Learn the terminology of classification, chain and link, sought link, unsought link and false link as used in the Code's examples. Do not assume that each number fragment automatically creates an index entry: follow the specific chain-procedure rules and examples.

Part K's decision sequence

  • KA–KC: learn the terminology of classification and the chain procedure, then work through the examples of chain and link.
  • KD–KE: decide the class-index heading and learn the prescribed rendering of that heading.
  • KF–KG: learn the structure and study the worked examples of Class Index Entries.
  • KH–KL: consult the chapters on classic works, commission reports, homonyms in class-index headings and feature headings where relevant.
  • KZD: check the dictionary-catalogue modifications where that alternative is being used.

05

Build main entries and added entries

Tracing, cross-reference and book-index entries · Parts L–M

Card craft

One document, several access points

The Main Entry is a specific entry giving maximum information about the whole document. Other specific or general entries are normally derived from it, but each entry type supports a different search path.

  • Main Entry: contains the maximum information prescribed for the whole document. Part M treats source, sections, leading section, heading, title, note, accession number and tracing.
  • Tracing Section: on the back of the Main Entry card, it indicates the added entries made for the document. It is the inventory that should match the entries you actually prepare.
  • Cross Reference Entry (classified catalogue): consult Part M's relevant rule to relate the document to its class/locus as prescribed.
  • Book Index Entry (BIE): a specific added word-entry for a particular document. It gives a heading, a specification of the document, and its call number/index-number section under the relevant rule.
  • Cross Reference Index Entry (CRIE): a general added entry directing from one word or set of words to another, often from an alternative form to a preferred heading. Part L includes alternative name, variant-form, pseudonym/real-name, editor-of-series and generic-name entries.

Use this workflow for an ordinary single-volume simple book

  1. Inspect the source pages and distinguish facts from decisions.
  2. Resolve authorship (Part G), then choose and render the heading (Parts H/J).
  3. Write the Main Entry sections using Part M's applicable rule.
  4. Determine which additional access points are supported and required; consult K, L and M.
  5. Record all added entries in tracing; write the entries separately and cross-check them against the tracing.

Quick check: which entry is a specific added word-entry for one particular document?

Source map: Part L, pp. 337–354; Part M, pp. 355–412. Key Part M chapters: MA–MH, MJ and MK.

06

Handle complex books and periodicals

Composite, multi-volume and changing publications · Parts N and P

Special cases

Classify the document type first

Part N covers ordinary composite books, artificial composite books and multi-volumed books. In the Code's terminology, a composite book contains two or more contributions, each with its own title, that do not form one continuous exposition. An ordinary composite book has a generic title for the contributions collectively; an artificial composite book lacks such a common generic title and may have been assembled by a publisher or library.

Part P treats periodical-publication complexities: simple periodical publications, irregularities in volume-number, interrupted publication, changes of title and sponsor, amalgamation, splitting up, supplements and combinations of these complexities.

Practical workflow

  1. Identify the work type: simple book, composite book, multi-volumed work or continuing publication.
  2. Write down the publication history before deciding how to catalogue it.
  3. Identify exactly what happened: numbering irregularity, interruption, title/sponsor change, merge, split or supplement.
  4. Consult the matching N or P chapter and its examples before preparing the main entry or related entries.

The terminology section distinguishes periodicals made up of distinct contributions from serials that generally provide a similar type of information in a recurring pattern. Use the Code's definitions and the matching rules on borderline cases.

Quick check: a periodical changes title and sponsor. Which Part should you consult first for its periodical-specific treatment?

Source map: Part N, pp. 413–432; Part P, pp. 433–488.

07

Connect CCC to the wider bibliographic system

Union catalogues, bibliography, indexes, abstracts and capstone · Parts Q–W

Integration

Finish the book map

  • Part Q: Union Catalogue of Books — coverage, modifications, main entry, library number and card.
  • Part R: Union Catalogue of Periodical Publications — coverage, users, physical form, main/other entries, abbreviations, contractions, typography, layout, pagination and card technique.
  • Part S: National Bibliography — definition, value, physical form, rule modifications, entries, arrangement and printed unit cards.
  • Part T: Indexing Periodical — value, coverage, rule modifications, entries and classified index.
  • Part U: Abstracting Periodical — value, canons, personnel, rule modifications, main entry and examples.
  • Part V: incunabula and non-book materials for consideration.
  • Part W: glossary, bibliographical references and index — navigation and reference aids for the entire Code.

Capstone: catalogue one real item

  1. Collect the title-page/overflow information and other relevant source data.
  2. Decide the author/responsibility and heading, citing the section consulted.
  3. Draft the Main Entry using the appropriate sections and style rules.
  4. If the exercise requires a Class Index Entry, derive it by the Part K procedure.
  5. Prepare required Book Index and cross-reference entries under Parts L–M.
  6. Check that tracing matches the added entries; review filing and any special document-type rules.

For union catalogues, national bibliography, indexing and abstracting, use the relevant Part rather than forcing the case into the ordinary simple-book workflow.

Quick check: a work is being catalogued as part of a national bibliography. Which Part should you check first?

Source map: Parts Q–W, pp. 489–623. Use Part W's glossary and index as navigation tools.
